Results on the held-out engines

These numbers come from the 707 NASA test engines, which the models and thresholds never saw. Every threshold was chosen on the training engines only. The headline: the Plan maintenance alert caught all 173 engines that reached their maintenance window inside the data, with a median of 32 cycles of warning, and fired early on 3 engines.

How an alert is judged

Each engine gets one decision per tier: the first time that alert fires. That moment is compared with the engine's real failure cycle. Every tier has a window. An alert that fires inside the window, with enough lead time to act, is a catch. An alert that fires before the window opens is false, because it would pull a healthy engine into the shop.

The NASA test trajectories stop before failure, so two outcomes exist only there. Quiet, correct means the engine never got near failure in the observed data and no alert fired: a true negative. Too soon to tell means the engine entered its window but the data ends before the window closes, with no alert yet. Those engines are neither caught nor missed, and they are left out of the detection rate.

Choosing the PLAN threshold

The PLAN alert fires when predicted RUL drops to a threshold T. Raising T buys warning time but starts flagging engines that still have more than 60 cycles left. I swept T on cross-validated predictions, scored each value as 10 × (late + missed) + 1 × false, and took the middle of the lowest-cost range so the setting isn't perched on the edge where it tips over.

RMSE is in cycles against RUL capped at 125, the usual convention for this benchmark. "Last cycle" is the standard published C-MAPSS metric. The NASA score penalizes late predictions more than early ones; lower is better.

Method

  • Operating regimes. FD002 and FD004 sensors swing with altitude, Mach and throttle. Each cycle is assigned to one of six regimes by clustering the three operating settings, and every sensor is standardized within its regime against healthy early-life cycles.
  • Anomaly score. Each engine is compared with its own first 20 cycles, which removes manufacturing and initial-wear differences between engines. The score is the Mahalanobis distance of that deviation, using the noise covariance of still-healthy cycles, smoothed with an EWMA. The threshold is the 99.5th percentile on early-life training cycles. Alerts are suppressed during the 20-cycle warm-up.
  • Remaining-life model. Histogram gradient boosting on 49 past-only features: regime-normalized sensors, 10- and 30-cycle rolling means, 20-cycle slopes, the anomaly score and its trend, and cycle count. Target is RUL capped at 125.
  • Validation. Five-fold cross-validation grouped by engine on the 709 run-to-failure training engines, with all preprocessing refit inside each fold. Thresholds come from these out-of-fold predictions; the NASA test set is scored once at the end.
  • Leakage checks. No label-derived column reaches the model, and deleting an engine's future cycles leaves its earlier predictions identical. Outcome counts were recomputed by separate code and matched in all 24 dataset × tier × split cells.

Limits

  • Cross-validated results are slightly optimistic because the PLAN threshold was tuned on them. The held-out test results carry no such bias, and they agree.
  • The windows (150, 60 and 30 cycles) and the 10:1 cost ratio are stated assumptions. A real fleet would set them from shop lead times and the cost of an unscheduled removal.
  • WATCH works cleanly on FD001 but fires with under 30 cycles left on 12–32% of engines in FD002–FD004. Regime normalization and the second fault mode both add noise, which pushes the healthy threshold up. PLAN is unaffected.
  • C-MAPSS is simulated: every engine fails by gradual degradation, with no sensor dropouts or sudden failures. Real data would need handling for both.
